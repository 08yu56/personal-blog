Leading a team that wasn't on the same page yet

The skill gap nobody said out loud

Our IT Lead works as an IT specialist, does social work on the side, and actually built a chatbot before, back in polytechnic. The rest of us, myself included, had never built anything like this. Not a small gap. A genuine "he can picture the whole system, we're picturing pieces of it" gap.

Nobody says that out loud at the start. It's not that it isn't real from day one, it's that it stays quiet, and quietly gets worse, until suddenly you're almost halfway through the project and it's still sitting there, unresolved.

We were all not on the same page

Nobody actually said that line in our group chat. It's just what came to me afterward, once I looked back at that thread properly and realised what had actually happened. In the screenshot, purple is me. I was the one asking whether the engine actually needed to be working for our presentation, since there was this looming question of chatbot engine versus prototype and wireframes, and whether a working version was actually expected or if the visuals alone would do. Someone else answered yes. A third person wasn't even sure we could guarantee that. Three different people, three different mental models of what "ready to present" even meant, all talking past each other in the same thread.

It wasn't anyone being careless. It was five people with wildly different starting points trying to build the same thing without a shared map yet.

What I actually did about it

I'd done leadership roles before (stage manager, president's assistant), but those teams were already pulling in roughly the same direction. This was the first time I was leading something where the team itself was the hard part, not the task. So I built a process, mostly because I needed one for myself as much as for anyone else.

1. Collect what the supervisor actually said

Every time we actually get a chance to meet, I write down our supervisor's feedback as closely to their actual words as I can, including the vague or half-formed parts, not just the clean action items. We don't get to meet weekly, so I make the most of whatever time we do get.

2. Hand it to AI to structure, not to decide

I run the raw feedback through AI to turn it into something specific and actionable, the same way you'd ask someone to turn a vague brief into a clear spec. I think of it like this:

How I think about the summarising step

Supervisor says "I want a pink cake with big cherries and a jam in the middle."
What I ask AI to turn that into Make pink cake
  • Timing When's it actually needed by? Tomorrow, or is there room to get it right?
  • Big cherries What kind?
  • Jam filling What kind of jam? What's actually the best option here?
  • Pink fondant What kind of pink? How light or dark?

That second layer of questions is the part that actually makes the list usable. A bullet like "big cherries" isn't a task yet, it's a label. The follow-up questions are what turn it into something someone can actually pick up without having to come back and ask me what I meant.

Two other questions come up naturally too, even though they're not part of the formal list I run for every task: who's actually going to do it, and where it fits in the bigger flow. I don't rank by these the way I do with time or difficulty yet, but they still shape a lot of the quieter decisions, like who I end up handing something to, or whether it needs to happen before something else. I just haven't formalised them into the process yet. Still figuring that part out.

3. Turn the list into priorities, not just tasks

Once everything is written clearly, I ask AI to help rank it against a few questions I apply to every item:

  • Time How urgent is this? Does it block other work, or can it wait?
  • Importance Is this foundational, or could we neglect it without the product falling apart?
  • Manpower How many of us does this realistically need?
  • Dependencies Does this depend on something else finishing first, or is someone else blocked waiting on it?
  • Difficulty Does this need a particular skill that only one or two of us actually have?

That ranked list becomes the backbone of the week: not a flat to-do list, but an ordered one, so when something has to give, we already know what's allowed to move.

Back to the cake for a second, since it's the clearest way I can explain "importance" to myself. If the batter doesn't rise properly, or the middle never bakes through, nothing else about that cake matters, it's failed at the structural level, no amount of good icing saves it. But if the pink ends up a slightly different shade, or the cherries aren't quite as glazed as hoped, the cake still works. It's not wrong, it's just not perfect. So for every task, I ask myself the same question: is this the batter rising, or is this the icing? Only one of those is allowed to slip.

In practice, the ranking mostly decides what gets done and when, not always who does it. Sometimes I let people pick what they want to work on. Other times, especially for the harder or more skill-specific items, I assign it directly based on who's actually strongest at that particular thing.

4. Break it into a weekly cycle

We work in Agile, which for us means every Thursday is a reset point. This is the actual cycle I proposed and we now run every week:

01

Review last week: what was assigned, what's done, what's still in progress, what didn't get done and why.

02

Surface blockers: questions for our supervisor, technical problems, unclear requirements, differences in opinion.

03

Discuss changes: anything the assessor or supervisor has asked us to shift, and what that does to the plan.

04

Assign next week: decide exactly what needs doing before the next Thursday, who owns it, and what "done" actually means.

What that actually looked like for one week, roles anonymised, in the order it actually had to happen:

  1. Teammate A goes through all the CSV data first, flags which book links are broken, then finds and tests at least 3 alternate links for each.
  2. Teammate B does the same thing for videos: goes through the data, flags the broken links, then finds and tests at least 3 alternates.

It looks simple laid out like that, but getting the timing right in real time wasn't.

Each step gets its own deadline, not one deadline for the whole week, since flagging has to happen before the alternate links can even be searched for. Working out that kind of dependency chain, and assigning realistic deadlines around it, has honestly been one of the harder parts of this whole process. Compared to other group projects I've done in school so far, this is a genuinely bigger scale, more moving parts, more people waiting on other people, and I'm still learning how to plan around that properly.

5. Make the meeting hands-on, not just a status update

The part I'm proudest of proposing: after every Thursday meeting, we stay and actually start the week's tasks together, right there. Our IT Lead is already in the room, so if anyone hits something they don't understand, he can show them on the spot, and increasingly, we've started helping each other too. Nobody's required to stay once they're confident in what they're doing. You leave when you're ready, not when the clock says so.

The point was never to make people stay longer. It was to make sure nobody left confused.

Process isn't the whole story

Honestly, most people are fine with the workload itself. What actually makes or breaks a team is the environment around it: attitude, how people treat each other, the general mood in the room. Small things help more than they should.

I haven't actually started doing this yet, but it's something I want to try: bringing snacks or drinks to our Thursday sessions. It sounds like a tiny thing to bother writing down, but I think it says something. It's one of those things I wish I'd started from the very beginning instead of only thinking of it now, this far in.

Seeing the rest of the team take it from here

The part I didn't plan for, and the part that actually made me happiest, happened with one of my quieter teammates. She started out in a fairly non-technical role early on, mostly checking broken links and cleaning up our book and video data. Somewhere along the way, without anyone assigning it to her, she became one of the most proactive people on the team, and started picking up chatbot intelligence work too. Then one day, she went ahead and froze our system architecture unprompted, writing it out clearly enough that all five of us could finally work against the same definition of "our database" for the first time.

That message mattered more to me than it probably looked like from the outside. It meant the gap I'd noticed early on wasn't just something I was managing from the top. It had become something the whole team was actively fixing, in their own way, without being told to.

Our actual priority list (for now)

This is the real breakdown we're working against right now, not a simplified version. I put this together using the exact process above: pulling from supervisor feedback, running it through AI to structure, then ranking it against time, importance, manpower, dependencies, and difficulty.

Highest priority: core system

1. Chatbot intelligence

  • Understand normal and broken English in progress
  • Understand children's intent
  • Rephrase and clarify questions using NLP in progress
  • Handle follow-up questions in progress
  • Maintain conversation context and history in progress
  • Understand when a new question relates to something previously discussed

2. Make the recommendation engine accurate

  • Verify the database in progress
  • Improve best-match accuracy in progress
  • Check topic and relevance against age
  • Retrieve alternative books when the first match isn't suitable in progress
  • Test recommendation quality in progress

3. Finalise database and data architecture

  • Decide one database vs. multiple databases
  • Finalise the database schema
  • Determine what kind of data needs to be stored
  • Determine how recommendation and history data is stored
  • Determine how unavailable books are tracked
  • Determine how the database stays updated, likely via an update script

High priority: make the AI education-aware

4. Syllabus and learning-level intelligence

  • Use the child's age as the primary indicator for content suitability
  • Check whether content is suitable for the age group
  • Handle questions that are too advanced or inappropriate for the target age
  • Use age to personalise the chatbot's explanations and recommendations
  • Automatically update the child's age as they grow

5. NLP architecture and demonstration

  • Show the different processing tiers: understand the question, check age and suitability, search the database, select a recommendation, generate the response
  • Show the NLP, database, and recommendation flow end to end
  • Demonstrate what the system actually does
  • Show how a child's question gets processed, step by step

One piece of priority 3 (how we stop books from just vanishing without a trace) is already scripted out:

1. Read the latest CSV export. 2. Check each book's unique ID against what's already in MySQL. 3. New book, insert it. 4. Existing book, info changed, update it. 5. Book no longer in the latest dataset, mark unavailable, don't delete. 6. Log it so admin gets notified.

A personal reflection, for now

Why this has felt hard

Honestly, this has been difficult. Not the workload itself, the people part. We're genuinely different: different skill levels, different ways of understanding the same instruction, different paces of getting there. I don't think that difficulty fully goes away just because a process now exists on paper.

Five different apples

In my past roles, like stage managing or being a president's assistant, if I said "apple," everyone around me pictured the same apple. We were already aligned on what the finished thing was supposed to look like, so my job was really just getting us there together.

On this project, because of the skill disparity between us, that wasn't true. If I said "apple," I'd get back five different versions of it, five different ideas of what the finished product should even look like. The challenge was never getting everyone to agree on one apple from day one. It was taking five genuinely different apples and compiling them into a single one all of us could actually look toward.

Starting from nothing

Thinking about it more, I think part of why this has felt harder than my past leadership roles is that we're building something from nothing. In roles like stage managing or being a president's assistant, I already knew the specs, the details, everything going in, so planning was really just executing against something known. This time, there's no existing blueprint to plan against. We're figuring out what the thing even is as we go, and that changes what planning actually means.

What actually helped

What actually helped wasn't the plan itself, it was the sitting-down-and-doing-the-work-together part. Watching someone go from unsure to capable in the same room, in real time, did more for us than any timeline could on its own. This whole approach is a role I'm still figuring out as I go, and it's rough around the edges. But it's helping, and right now, that's enough.

Being present, not backseat

One thing I've realised along the way: being a good coordinator isn't about planning from the sidelines. If I actually want to lead this well, I have to be there, doing the work too, not just assigning it and stepping back. When something's heavy, I take it on as well, not only because it needs doing, but because I'm still a member of this team, not just the person managing it.

Thinking beyond the week

I'm also trying to get better at thinking further ahead, not just week to week, and that one is its own kind of hard. Honestly, the most I can reliably plan right now is a week at a time. When I try to plan further out, "advanced" for me currently just means about three weeks ahead, not months. I don't know yet if that's a scale problem, a me problem, or just where a first project like this naturally sits. Still working it out.

This page will keep changing as the project does. That's the whole point of calling it work in progress.

Jasmine April Aulia Ng, Vice Lead, Final Year Project, University of Wollongong
